Don't Design Life Around Work

The Economics of Time Misallocation

Work is not the enemy. Misallocating it is.

From the preface

Let’s price the trade you’ve been making for free.

A colleague of mine — a rising officer at one of the banks I used to work for — was once praised in a staff meeting for answering emails at eleven at night, three nights in a row, during his daughter's exam week.

He took it as a compliment. I remember thinking: nobody priced what that actually cost him.

That is the trade this book is about. Not the tired argument between "hard work" and "laziness" — that one is settled and boring. The real trade is between two things economists take seriously and most professionals never do: time as a scarce resource, and work as only one of the places you can spend it.

You already know the vocabulary. Opportunity cost. Diminishing returns. Misallocation. You have probably used these words in a meeting to describe someone else's bad decision.

This book asks you to turn them on your own calendar.

Here is the uncomfortable part. If you have ever skipped a birthday for "one more call," moved an anniversary dinner for a deadline whose name you cannot even remember six months later, or told yourself "next year will be different" more than once — you already know how this story ends if nothing changes. You simply never had a name for what you were doing to yourself.

Part V of this book hands you the discipline for reallocating your time the way a good portfolio manager reallocates capital — deliberately, and without guilt. But before you get the discipline, you need to see the misallocation clearly, in numbers you cannot argue with. That is Parts I through IV.

My colleague got his promotion. He also missed the exam week. Both sentences are true, and this book exists because most of us were never taught to ask whether that trade was worth it — or whether we even chose it on purpose.

Work is not the enemy. Misallocating it is.

Chapter 1

The Calendar That Ran Her Life

Elena is thirty-four. She has a promotion, a corner desk, and a calendar that starts filling at 6 a.m. and doesn't stop until the last Slack message goes quiet, usually past ten.

She didn't plan it this way. Nobody sits down at twenty-two and decides, I will let my job choose my sleep, my friendships, and the shape of my week. It happened one reasonable trade-off at a time. Skip the gym, there's a deadline. Move the dinner with friends, there's a client call. Postpone the vacation, there's a promotion cycle. Each decision made sense on its own. None of them were ever compared to what they cost.

By the time Elena noticed, the arithmetic had already been decided for her. Work was the fixed point. Everything else — health, relationships, rest, the slow projects that actually make a life feel like hers — had become the flexible variable, rearranged around whatever the job needed that week.

She would have told you, if you'd asked, that she was building a life. What she was actually doing was building a job, and renting out the rest of her life to pay for it.

It's an easy trap to fall into, because work is the only part of life with a fixed schedule, a visible scoreboard, and someone else keeping track of whether you showed up. Health has no boss checking attendance. Relationships send no deadline reminder. Rest never invoices you for skipping it. So when something has to give, it's never the job — it's whichever part of life is quiet enough not to complain yet.

That's the whole trick, and it's a costly one. Economists have a word for what it costs: opportunity cost, the value of the next-best thing you gave up to get what you chose. Every hour spent finishing a deck has a price, and the price isn't the salary. It's whatever that hour would have been worth instead — as sleep, as a conversation with someone you love, as a walk with nowhere to be.

Here's the part that makes it worse than an ordinary bad trade. Money is renewable. You can earn more of it, save it, borrow against tomorrow's. Time is not. A Sunday afternoon spent answering email cannot be re-earned next quarter — it's gone, permanently, at a fixed rate of sixty minutes an hour, whether you're rich or broke. And the return on trading it for income shrinks the harder you push. The first raise that lifts a family out of scarcity changes everything about how they live. The fifth raise after that, chasing a life already comfortable, changes very little — researchers who study happiness and income have found it rises with money only up to a point, after which more of it barely moves the needle for anyone but the most anxious among us. Yet the trap keeps optimizing for the resource with shrinking returns, income, while spending down the one resource that never grows back, time, as if it cost nothing at all.

It has a name now, this particular way of running a life into the ground. Doctors and researchers who track it call it burnout, and they define it with a precision that should unsettle anyone who wears exhaustion like a badge: energy that keeps draining, a creeping distance from the very job you once cared about, and — this is the part people miss — declining effectiveness at the work you sacrificed everything else for. Burnout doesn't just cost you your evenings. Eventually it costs the very output the extra hours were spent chasing.

And it isn't a handful of unlucky people. Ask around the world and you'll find something close to half of working adults saying they're struggling rather than thriving, and something close to two in five reporting real stress on any given day. Whole economies run this way — some countries clock over two hundred more working days a year than others doing comparable work, and nobody would call the difference a difference in ambition. It's a difference in how deliberately a life was designed.

The fix isn't to work less out of protest. It's to allocate time the way you'd allocate capital — on purpose, against a budget, with the fixed and flexible variables put back in their correct order.

Try this. Take a real week from your calendar, the one that already happened, not the one you intended. Sort every block of time into four piles: Income, Health, Relationships, and everything else — rest, growth, the things with no deadline attached. Add up the hours. Most people who do this honestly find that one pile has quietly claimed most of a life that was supposed to be shared four ways.

Then do the part that actually changes something. Pick one fixed, non-negotiable block this week for whichever pile isn't Income — a specific hour, on a specific day, defended with the same seriousness as a client call. Not "I'll try to exercise more." Tuesday, 6 p.m., gym, immovable.

Designing life around work is the default setting. Designing work around life is a decision — and like every good decision, it only happens on purpose. Otherwise, someone else's calendar will make it for you.

Work will always ask for more of your life than it deserves — it never has to ask twice if you never say no.

Design the calendar on purpose, or someone else's priorities will design it for you.
